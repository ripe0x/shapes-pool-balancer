## Claim versus demonstrated domain

**Accepted within stated bounds:** a hostile curated ERC-721 that permits an out-of-band transfer from FWA can leave FWA's stored listing active and Balancer's checkpoint admitting purchases. The test first lists the token normally, then calls the mock's public `seize(from=pool, to=stranger, id=123)`. It asserts the real ERC-721 `ownerOf(123)` is the stranger while FWA `listings(ghostListing).status == 1`, FWA `activeListingCount == Balancer activeIds.length`, and Balancer `admissionStatus() == 0`. This confirms that neither FWA's stored inventory totals nor Balancer's sequence/version checkpoint detects this collection-level custody change in that state. The `SeizableNFT` backdoor is deliberately stronger than a normal ERC-721: its public `seize` calls internal `_transfer` without owner/operator authorization. This is a valid hostile-collection model, not proof an ordinary ERC-721 has that power.

The funded `BuyerAgent` then pays the live FWA quote, and its ETH balance falls by the quoted `total`. The test supplies a VRF word selecting the ghost listing through the fork's coordinator impersonation; FWA records that listing ID in the acquisition, its delivery attempt fails, and the listing enters status **6, DeliveryPending**. Balancer `performUpkeep` succeeds; Balancer again reports status 0 with the remaining inventory. After seven days plus one second, the buyer contract calls `refundFailedDelivery` and `withdrawAcquisitionRefund`. The returned refund is exactly the quoted FWA acquisition `fee`, and the buyer contract balance is exactly its starting balance minus the quoted `vrf` service fee, excluding gas. The stranger still owns token 123. The supplied `.out` reports **one test passed, zero failed**; it does not record the shell command or a call trace. The source assertions and reported result are the evidence for this sequence.

**Narrowed harm:** this is a seven-day acquisition-fee lock and nonrefundable VRF fee loss to a buyer whose random draw selects the ghost, plus the creator's loss of control over token 123 at the time of the test. It is not a permanent loss of the buyer's FWA acquisition fee, not a demonstrated Balancer ETH theft, and not proof that the ghost is selected on every purchase. The test forces a valid VRF branch; it does not show an outsider can choose Chainlink's live word or estimate selection frequency. The hostile collection's `seize` call itself has no FWA purchase fee; the seizing actor pays only its transaction gas in a live execution. An unrelated buyer pays the VRF fee if it later draws the ghost. The test does not establish the seized NFT's market value or attacker profit. Because `seize` is public in this mock, the asserted stranger ownership is not durable against another caller re-seizing the token; a realistic admin-only seizure would need its own authority model.

## Other rights and exact gaps

- The PoC asserts Balancer returns to status 0 after upkeep, so the ghost does not prevent that specific maintenance call or policy admission for the remaining listings. It does **not** execute a second buyer purchase, withdraw the normal curated NFT, unwind the Shape, claim Balancer surplus, or try `recoverCuratedNFT` after refund. Those rights cannot be claimed exercised by this test. The earlier `HostileBuyer.t.sol` exercises unrelated exits for a *transfer-blocking* NFT, which is a different hostile behavior and not a direct proof for out-of-band seizure.
- The test does not assert FWA listing status **7** after `refundFailedDelivery` or Balancer item state **PENDING** after upkeep. The successful refund withdrawal and known FWA source strongly support the refund path, but those specific storage states are not directly checked. A precise follow-up would add those assertions and attempt creator recovery, expected to fail or remain impossible while FWA no longer owns the NFT. This follow-up is needed before claiming the creator's Balancer recovery path is blocked after the refund.
- The test checks FWA active-listing count but not the independently stored FWA weight and weighted-backing totals after seizure. Since those are stored FWA bookkeeping and the purchase completes, a mismatch is plausible, but an exact accounting claim needs their before/after assertions. It also does not test a collection that lies about `ownerOf` or re-seizes during FWA delivery; those are separate variants.
- Balancer cannot generally force an arbitrary ERC-721 collection to preserve FWA custody; a one-time `ownerOf` read would not prevent later seizure. The specific product question is whether admission status is promised as live custody verification, or only a checkpoint against FWA's own accounting. Current source and test support the latter. Any buyer-facing protection proposal needs to account for the 100,000-gas purchase hook and a collection that can lie or move the token after a check.

**Disposition:** accepted for the pinned hostile-collection seizure and forced random-selection sequence; broader incidence, durable attacker gain, post-refund creator recovery, and unrelated exits remain unproven. Do not promote the test to an outsider-alone attack on standard ERC-721s or a universal buyer-loss claim.
